# 02 — Behavioral design: engagement, enjoyment, and extraction

## Core idea

The same design mechanisms can produce enjoyment or harm. Variable rewards, difficulty, scarcity, social status, and repetition appear in arcade games, roguelikes, competitive multiplayer games, and gacha systems. Mechanism alone does not determine ethics.

A player may genuinely enjoy a system that also exploits their vulnerabilities. **Enjoyment and exploitation can coexist.** Consequently, intent alone is insufficient; examine incentives, consent, effects, and the player's practical ability to disengage.

## Four lenses

| Lens | Question |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | Which tendencies does the interface activate? |
| Intent | What are designers trying to cause? |
| Incentives | What behavior generates revenue or organizational success? |
| Consequences | What happens to agency, enjoyment, time, money, and welfare? |

A roguelike can use uncertain rewards to support mastery; a gacha system can use uncertain rewards to create real in-game utility **and** encourage excessive spending. Cosmetic purchases can support self-expression while exploiting social pressure or artificial scarcity.

## Two feedback loops

**Value creation:** enjoyable experience → satisfaction → voluntary support → investment in better experiences.

**Extraction:** engineered friction or pressure → spending → reward for increasing pressure → further friction.

Most real products contain a mixture. A useful test: *Can the system's commercial performance improve while its users become worse off?* This is a warning sign, not a complete ethical verdict.

## Practical design checks

- Separate voluntary engagement from compulsive return.
- Study regret, loss of control, and financial harm, not only retention.
- Make prices, odds, and consequences understandable.
- Avoid exploiting vulnerabilities or deliberately creating problems primarily to sell relief.
- Include user-reported welfare and autonomy in success criteria.

## Related fields

Human–computer interaction, persuasive technology, behavioral economics, dark patterns/deceptive design, self-determination theory, ethics of design, game studies, consumer protection.

## HVSM connection

Treat the player or customer as an agent whose capacity for choice matters, not simply as an engagement metric or revenue source.
